Parallel Lines

Angle pairs made by parallel lines and a transversal, solving for x, two-column proofs, and the logic behind them. Learn it, then test yourself.

The big idea

Eight angles, only two sizes

When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, it makes eight angles. But there are only two different sizes: every acute angle is equal, every obtuse angle is equal, and one of each always adds up to 180°.

Congruent equal measures

  • Corresponding
  • Alternate interior
  • Alternate exterior
  • Vertical

Supplementary add up to 180°

  • Same-side interior
  • Same-side exterior
  • Linear pair

The arrow marks (›) on lines ℓ and m mean they are parallel. These rules only work when the lines are parallel, except vertical angles and linear pairs, which work for any two lines that cross.

The shortcut

With parallel lines, if two angles are both acute or both obtuse, they're equal. If one is acute and one is obtuse, they add to 180°. You still need the pair's name for proofs, but this tells you which equation to write.

Lines & angles

Angle pairs

A transversal is a line that crosses two or more lines. Angles between the two lines are interior. Angles outside them are exterior. Each pair's name tells you where its two angles are.

Corresponding angles

Congruent

Where: the same spot at each intersection (both upper-left, both lower-right, and so on).

Look for: an F shape. Pairs: ∠1 & ∠5, ∠2 & ∠6, ∠3 & ∠7, ∠4 & ∠8.

Alternate interior angles

Congruent

Where: between the lines, on opposite sides of the transversal.

Look for: a Z shape. Pairs: ∠3 & ∠6, ∠4 & ∠5.

Alternate exterior angles

Congruent

Where: outside the lines, on opposite sides of the transversal.

Pairs: ∠1 & ∠8, ∠2 & ∠7.

Vertical angles

Always congruent

Where: across from each other at the same intersection. They make an X.

Pairs: ∠1 & ∠4, ∠2 & ∠3, ∠5 & ∠8, ∠6 & ∠7. True for any two crossing lines.

Same-side interior angles

Supplementary

Where: between the lines, on the same side of the transversal. Also called consecutive interior angles.

Look for: a C or U shape. Pairs: ∠3 & ∠5, ∠4 & ∠6.

Same-side exterior angles

Supplementary

Where: outside the lines, on the same side of the transversal. Also called consecutive exterior angles.

Pairs: ∠1 & ∠7, ∠2 & ∠8.

Linear pair

Always supplementary

Where: side by side at the same intersection, sharing a side. Together they make a straight line (180°).

Pairs: ∠1 & ∠2, ∠1 & ∠3, ∠2 & ∠4, and so on. True for any two crossing lines.

Kinds of lines

Parallel

In the same plane and never meet. Written ℓ ∥ m.

Perpendicular

Meet at a right angle (90°). Written a ⊥ b. The small square marks the right angle.

Intersecting

Cross at exactly one point, making two pairs of vertical angles.

Skew

Not in the same plane, and never meet. Like the two highlighted edges of a box.

Perpendicular Transversal Theorem

If a line is perpendicular to one of two parallel lines, it is perpendicular to the other one too.

Transitive Property of Parallel Lines

If a ∥ b and b ∥ c, then a ∥ c.

Algebra with angles

Solving for x

Most problems give two angles as expressions like (5x + 14)°. The angle pair tells you which equation to write.

  1. Name the pairCorresponding? Same-side interior? Find where the two angles are.
  2. Pick the equationCongruent pair: set them equal. Supplementary pair: add them and set the sum to 180.
  3. Solve for xGet the x terms on one side and the numbers on the other.
  4. Answer the questionIf it asks for an angle, plug x back in. Then check: acute angles should be under 90°.

Example 1 · congruent pair

Alternate interior angles

Lines ℓ and m are parallel. Find x and the measure of each angle.

  1. The angles are between the lines, on opposite sides of t: alternate interior, so they're congruent.
  2. Set them equal: 3x + 17 = 5x − 9
  3. Subtract 3x from both sides, then add 9: 26 = 2x, so x = 13.
  4. Plug in: 3(13) + 17 = 56°. Check the other one: 5(13) − 9 = 56°. ✓

x = 13, and both angles are 56°

Example 2 · supplementary pair

Same-side interior angles

Lines ℓ and m are parallel. Find x and both angles.

  1. Both angles are between the lines, on the same side of t: same-side interior, so they add to 180°.
  2. Add them: (4x + 12) + (2x + 6) = 180
  3. Combine like terms: 6x + 18 = 180, so 6x = 162 and x = 27.
  4. Plug in: 4(27) + 12 = 120° and 2(27) + 6 = 60°. They add to 180°. ✓

x = 27, and the angles are 120° and 60°

Example 3 · find a different angle

Corresponding angles, then a linear pair

Lines ℓ and m are parallel. Find m∠1.

  1. The two expressions are in the same spot (upper-left) at each intersection: corresponding, so they're equal.
  2. 6x − 2 = 4x + 22, so 2x = 24 and x = 12.
  3. Those angles are 6(12) − 2 = 70°.
  4. ∠1 makes a linear pair with the 70° angle next to it, so m∠1 = 180° − 70° = 110°.

m∠1 = 110° (not 70°: always answer what was asked)

Example 4 · two crossing lines

Vertical angles

Find x. (No parallel lines needed: vertical angles are always equal.)

  1. The angles are across from each other at the same point: vertical, so they're congruent.
  2. 7x − 4 = 5x + 12, so 2x = 16 and x = 8.
  3. Each angle is 7(8) − 4 = 52°.

x = 8 (each angle is 52°)

Example 5 · angle bisector

A ray that splits an angle in half

BD bisects ∠ABC. Find m∠ABC.

  1. A bisector makes two congruent angles: 3x + 4 = 5x − 14.
  2. 18 = 2x, so x = 9.
  3. Each half is 3(9) + 4 = 31°.
  4. The whole angle is twice that: 2 × 31° = 62°.

m∠ABC = 62°

Common mistakes
  • Using the wrong equation. Setting supplementary angles equal, or adding congruent angles to 180. Name the pair first.
  • Stopping at x. If the question asks for an angle, plug x back in.
  • Answering the wrong angle. If the angle asked for isn't one of the expressions, use a vertical angle or linear pair to get to it (Example 3).
  • Not checking. Plug x into both expressions. Congruent pairs should match. Supplementary pairs should add to 180°.

Reasoning

Two-column proofs

A proof is a chain of statements, each backed by a reason. Start with the Given. End with what you need to Prove. Every step in between needs a definition, property, postulate, or theorem.

You know the lines are parallel

Use the theorem

Lines ∥ → angles. Example: “ℓ ∥ m, so ∠3 ≅ ∠6” uses the Alternate Interior Angles Theorem.

You want to prove lines are parallel

Use the converse

Angles → lines ∥. Example: “∠3 ≅ ∠6, so ℓ ∥ m” uses the Converse of the Alternate Interior Angles Theorem.

Reasons you'll use

Properties of equality

Addition / Subtraction / Multiplication / Division
Do the same thing to both sides. If x + 9 = 20, then x = 11 (Subtraction).
Substitution
Swap in something equal. If m∠1 = 50° and m∠1 + m∠2 = 180°, then 50° + m∠2 = 180°.
Distributive
2(x + 5) = 2x + 10
Reflexive · Symmetric · Transitive
a = a · If a = b, then b = a · If a = b and b = c, then a = c.

Properties of congruence

Reflexive
∠A ≅ ∠A
Symmetric
If ∠A ≅ ∠B, then ∠B ≅ ∠A.
Transitive
If ∠A ≅ ∠B and ∠B ≅ ∠C, then ∠A ≅ ∠C. This is how you link two congruences together.

Definitions (they work both ways)

Congruent angles
∠1 ≅ ∠2 means m∠1 = m∠2. Use it to switch between ≅ and =.
Angle bisector
A ray that splits an angle into two congruent angles.
Supplementary / Complementary
Two angles that add to 180° / to 90°.
Right angle · Perpendicular lines
A right angle measures 90°. Perpendicular lines meet at right angles.

Postulates & theorems

Angle Addition Postulate
If D is inside ∠ABC, then m∠ABD + m∠DBC = m∠ABC.
Linear Pair Postulate
A linear pair is supplementary. (Some books: Supplement Theorem.)
Vertical Angles Theorem
Vertical angles are congruent.
The parallel-line angle theorems and their converses
See the table in Angle pairs.
Proof tips
  • Write the Given first, one fact per line, with the reason “Given.”
  • Mark the diagram. Shade the angles you know are equal. The proof usually connects them in a chain.
  • Work backwards from the Prove. To prove lines parallel, you need a congruent (or supplementary) angle pair, so find which pair you can reach.
  • To combine “∠1 ≅ ∠3” and “∠1 ≅ ∠2” into “∠3 ≅ ∠2,” use the Transitive Property (or Substitution).

Linking two angle facts

Using a bisector inside a triangle

Some teachers want a separate line for “m∠1 = m∠2” (Definition of congruent angles) whenever you switch between ≅ and =. Ask what yours expects.

Reasoning

Conditional statements & logic

A conditional is an if-then statement. The “if” part is the hypothesis (p) and the “then” part is the conclusion (q).

If it is raining, then the ground is wet.

p = hypothesisq = conclusion→ “if… then”~ “not”↔ “if and only if”

Biconditional

Only when it works both ways

“An angle is a right angle if and only if it measures 90°” is true: both “if right, then 90°” and “if 90°, then right” are true.

“Two angles are congruent if and only if they are vertical” is false: congruent angles don't have to be vertical.

Counterexample

One example that breaks it

To show a conditional is false, find a case where the hypothesis is true but the conclusion is false.

“If two angles are congruent, then they are vertical angles.” Counterexample: alternate interior angles with parallel lines are congruent, but not vertical.

Laws of logic (deductive reasoning)

Law of Detachment

If p → q is true and p is true, then q is true.

If a figure is a square, then it has four right angles.

ABCD is a square.

∴ ABCD has four right angles.

Law of Syllogism

If p → q and q → r, then p → r.

If it snows, then school is canceled.

If school is canceled, then I sleep in.

∴ If it snows, then I sleep in.

Invalid arguments to watch for

Starting from the conclusion

If you study, then you pass.

Jordan passed.

∴ Jordan studied.

Invalid. Jordan could have passed another way. Knowing q doesn't tell you p.

Denying the hypothesis

If you study, then you pass.

Jordan didn't study.

∴ Jordan didn't pass.

Invalid. That's using the inverse, which isn't guaranteed.

A chain that doesn't link

If Sam practices, then he makes the team.

If Ava practices, then she makes the team.

∴ If Sam practices, then Ava makes the team.

Invalid. For syllogism, the conclusion of the first statement must be the hypothesis of the second.

Inductive reasoning

Look at examples or a pattern and make a conjecture (an educated guess). It could be wrong: one counterexample disproves it.

Deductive reasoning

Use facts, definitions, and the laws of logic to reach a conclusion that must be true. Proofs use deductive reasoning.

Glossary

Key words

Transversal
A line that crosses two or more lines at different points.
Parallel lines
Lines in the same plane that never intersect (∥).
Perpendicular lines
Lines that meet at right angles (⊥).
Skew lines
Lines that aren't in the same plane and never intersect.
Interior angles
Angles between the two lines a transversal crosses.
Exterior angles
Angles outside the two lines.
Corresponding angles
Same spot at each intersection. Congruent if the lines are parallel.
Alternate interior angles
Between the lines, opposite sides of the transversal. Congruent if the lines are parallel.
Alternate exterior angles
Outside the lines, opposite sides. Congruent if the lines are parallel.
Same-side interior angles
Between the lines, same side (consecutive interior). Supplementary if the lines are parallel.
Same-side exterior angles
Outside the lines, same side. Supplementary if the lines are parallel.
Vertical angles
Opposite angles formed by two crossing lines. Always congruent.
Linear pair
Two adjacent angles that form a straight line. Always supplementary.
Congruent (≅)
Exactly the same size. Congruent angles have equal measures.
Supplementary
Two angles that add up to 180°.
Complementary
Two angles that add up to 90°.
Angle bisector
A ray that divides an angle into two congruent angles.
Conditional
An if-then statement, p → q.
Hypothesis / Conclusion
The “if” part (p) / the “then” part (q).
Converse
q → p: switch the parts.
Inverse
~p → ~q: negate both parts.
Contrapositive
~q → ~p: switch and negate. Always as true as the original.
Biconditional
p ↔ q, “if and only if.” True only when it works both ways.
Negation
The opposite of a statement (~p, “not p”).
Counterexample
An example where the hypothesis is true but the conclusion is false.
Law of Detachment
p → q and p, so q.
Law of Syllogism
p → q and q → r, so p → r.
Postulate
A statement accepted as true without proof.
Theorem
A statement that has been proven.
